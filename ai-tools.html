Foundation · AI filter

A public chatbot is not a statement and not a counselor

Last reviewed October 1, 2026 · 14 minutes

Who this is for. Adults in the United States who already use, or are about to use, a general chatbot with a money question — explain a term, sort a list, or draft what to say on the phone.
Who should skip this. Anyone about to paste a statement, account number, Social Security number, password, or bank login into a chatbot. Stop and use the situation pages instead. Anyone who needs a court deadline or a bankruptcy decision: this page is not that help.

In one paragraph

A public AI chatbot can rewrite sentences and group numbers you already typed. It cannot see your lease, your state’s shutoff rules, your card’s real APR, or whether a collector’s letter is valid. It will sometimes invent a rule that sounds finished. People under money pressure get worse answers when the question is vague, because the model fills gaps with a typical story, not your file. Use FourHundred or an official page to name the situation first. Then, if you still want a model, paste page text or rounded categories — never account text.

If money is already short this month

Do not start with a chatbot.

  • Housing, utilities, food, or medicine at risk → I may not make this month’s bills
  • Collector or court paper → I am late, or in collections
  • Balance rising on an otherwise current card → My credit card balance is growing

A model may draft phone questions after you know which path you are on. It does not pick the path.

What this page is

AI is not the product. AI is not an adviser.

FourHundred pages are written and sourced by people. A model may help you read those pages faster. It may not choose a card, a loan, a settlement company, a bankruptcy filing, or an investment.

This page is a filter: what a general chatbot can do with money questions, the mistakes that show up most often, and three tools that stay on a short list because we can say what they cannot do. Listings are not endorsements. FourHundred does not rank chatbots.

What a public chatbot actually is

It predicts the next words from text like the text it has seen. That is useful for plain-language explanations. It is a poor substitute for a statement, a statute, or a person who can see your file.

It does not open your issuer app. It does not know whether your utility is in a winter shutoff state. It does not know if the letter on the table is a validation notice or a summons. If you do not put those facts in, it will guess — and the guess will still sound complete.

Bank and card apps now use their own chatbots. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau’s 2023 issue spotlight on chatbots in consumer finance found that those tools can handle simple lookups and then fail as the problem gets specific: wrong information, no way to reach a person, and fees that follow a bad answer. A public model is not your bank. The same failure shape still applies when you ask it which bill to pay.

Three tools, three risks

People say “the AI” and mean three different products. They fail in different ways. Do not treat them as one counselor.

1. The chatbot inside a bank or card app. In 2022, each of the ten largest U.S. commercial banks had a customer-service chatbot, and about 37 percent of the U.S. population had used a bank chatbot, according to the CFPB’s 2023 issue spotlight. Those tools can look up a balance. They get worse as the problem gets specific: wrong information, no path to a person, and fees that follow a bad answer. If the bot loops, ask for a human. A chatbot sentence is not a waiver of a due date.

2. A public chatbot you open yourself (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Grok, and similar). It does not see your lease, your statement, or your state shutoff rule unless you paste them. It drafts and sorts. It does not decide. A 2026 Harris Poll for NerdWallet found 26 percent of U.S. adults had asked a chatbot a personal-finance question; among those who acted on the answer, 29 percent said their finances got worse. That is a self-reported survey of 2,003 adults, not a Federal Reserve measure, and it is not a FourHundred Evidence card.

3. A chatbot with your accounts connected. In May 2026 OpenAI previewed a U.S. Pro feature that reads accounts through Plaid. Read-only still means balances, transactions, and debts in one place. If that login is stolen, the map is already drawn. Disconnecting can leave data in place for up to 30 days, per the product description. FourHundred does not use this path. Company claims about how many people ask finance questions are marketing, not a source on this page.

Correct use stays narrow: name the situation on a FourHundred or CFPB page, type rounded categories, draft a sentence you will say yourself, then check dates and APR on the statement. Incorrect use is asking which bill to pay, which card to open, whether to file, or pasting a statement so the model can “see everything.”

Allowed

A model may:

  • Explain a term that already appears on FourHundred ($400 cash or its equivalent, minimum payment, validation notice, 7-day cash map)
  • Compare two methods that are already published here, using only the text on those pages
  • Sort a spending list that contains no account numbers, Social Security numbers, or logins
  • Draft questions or a short script to ask a landlord, card issuer, hospital billing office, utility, or nonprofit counselor

After the draft exists, you send it. The model does not send it for you. It does not place a call. It does not click “pay.”

Not allowed

A model must not:

  • Tell you which product to buy, which account to open, or which company to pay first as if it could see your file
  • Tell you whether to file bankruptcy
  • Tell you what to invest in
  • Receive live bank, brokerage, or credit-account access
  • Receive a Social Security number, full account number, routing number, password, one-time code, or a complete statement in a public chatbot
  • Replace legal aid when a summons is on the table

If a tool needs those inputs to “work,” it does not belong on this page.

What you may type, and what you may not

Safe to type — rounded, no names attached:

  • Take-home pay this month as a round number
  • Rent or mortgage as a round number
  • A card minimum as a round number, without the last four digits of the account
  • Spending in categories: groceries, gas, medicine, subscriptions
  • The text of a FourHundred page, or a CFPB explainer, that you want rewritten in simpler words

Never type or upload

  • Social Security number
  • Account, routing, or card numbers
  • Passwords, PINs, one-time codes
  • A photo or PDF of a full statement, a check, or a government ID
  • A login screen, or a “connect your bank” button inside the chat

If the model asks for any of those to continue, stop. The job you wanted is outside the filter.

Errors that look finished

The useful warning is not “AI is sometimes wrong.” It is which kind of wrong shows up on money questions.

Invented rules. A model may state a clean exception that does not exist — for example, that moving to another state pauses a loan, or that a collector must stop if you dispute by text. Federal collection rules live on CFPB pages. Your statement warning box is the card’s own math. Prefer those over a fluent paragraph.

Wrong arithmetic presented as a plan. Multi-step payoff math is where general models break most often. Vendor tests in 2026 put hard, multi-part money questions in a high-error range. Treat any “you will be clear in 36 months if you pay $X” line as an example until you read the Minimum Payment Warning on your own statement. That box exists because federal rules require it.

The wrong country or the wrong year. Models mix U.S. cards with other countries’ tax and student-loan rules, and they keep old thresholds after a statute changes. If the answer does not name the United States and a source you can open, it is not ready to use.

A typical story instead of your file. Research on chatbot money advice finds that the quality of the answer moves with the quality of the question. A vague, anxious prompt gets a generic plan. People already under pressure are the ones most likely to type that prompt. That is why this site asks you to pick a situation page first.

A bank bot that will not hand you to a person. If the chatbot in your issuer’s app loops, ask for a human. CFPB’s chatbot spotlight is about that failure, not about whether the interface sounds kind.

Common uses to correct

“Tell me which bill to pay first.”
The model cannot see an eviction notice or a shutoff date. Use this month’s bills and the sheet. Then ask the model only to tighten a sentence you will read to a landlord or utility.

“Pick a balance-transfer card.”
Product choice is advice, and intro rates expire. Use CFPB’s consumer tools to understand what an intro period is. Do not let a chatbot name an issuer.

“Here is my PDF statement.”
Statements carry account numbers, addresses, and payees. Type four rounded figures instead.

“The bank bot said three days late is fine.”
A chatbot answer is not a waiver. The due date on the statement is. If the information looks wrong, get it in writing or speak to a person. CFPB takes complaints when an institution’s process blocks that.

“It ran the 36-month math, so the minimum is progress.”
The minimum keeps the account current. Most of a small payment can be interest. That is Playbook #1. Use the warning box on the statement, not the model’s schedule.

“I disputed it in the chat, so I do not owe it.”
A dispute is a process with a deadline and a written record. See I am late, or in collections and CFPB’s collector pages. A chatbot message is not a validation request.

“Connect the account so it can see everything.”
Live access is how a stolen login becomes a map of every balance. FourHundred pages work from numbers you write on paper. If a tool requires Plaid or a bank login to start, it is off this list.

How to use a general model with FourHundred

Do the situation path first. Then, if you still want a model, paste page text, not account text.

Copy one of these. Fill only the brackets. Leave balances and account numbers out.

Explain a term

I am reading FourHundred. Explain “cash or its equivalent” in the Federal Reserve $400 question in plain English. Do not tell me what I should do with my own money. Do not recommend a product.

Compare two published methods

Compare these two FourHundred pages using only the text I paste. List who each page is for and who should skip it. Do not add a third method. Do not recommend a card or a loan. Page A: [paste] Page B: [paste]

Draft questions, not a decision

Draft six questions I can ask a [card issuer / hospital billing office / landlord / utility] about a hardship arrangement. United States only. Do not tell me which offer to take. Do not ask for my account number. List any assumption you are making.

Sort a list without identifiers

Here is a list of spending categories and dollar amounts for one week. No account numbers. Group them into housing, food, work transport, medicine, and other. Do not tell me what to cancel.

Force the model to show its work

Rewrite the paragraph I paste in shorter sentences. Then list: (1) facts taken from my text, (2) assumptions you added, (3) anything I must check on an official U.S. page or on my statement. Do not add a product name.

If the model answers with a product name, a ticker, or “connect your bank,” stop. That answer is outside the filter.

Launch list (three)

1. A general-purpose chatbot

Examples people already have: ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Grok, and similar assistants. FourHundred does not rank them and does not need you to open a new account to use this site.

Use for: the allowed jobs above, with the prompts on this page.
Do not use for: live account connections, document filing, product picks, or legal strategy.
Cannot do: see your lease, your state court rules, or your full credit file. Treat any dollar figure it invents as an example until you check your statement.

2. CFPB Ask CFPB

Official consumer questions and answers from the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau.

https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/

Use for: debt collection notices, credit-card rules, mortgage servicer questions, what a collector has to tell you.
Do not use for: a personalized payoff plan.
Cannot do: call your issuer or change your due date. It explains the rule. You still make the call.

3. IRS Interactive Tax Assistant

Official walk-throughs for defined federal tax questions.

https://www.irs.gov/help/ita

Use for: whether a type of income is taxable, whether you can claim a standard deduction, basic filing-status questions the tool actually covers.
Do not use for: choosing an investment, or deciding whether to raid a retirement account to pay a collector.
Cannot do: replace a tax preparer when the fact pattern is messy, or tell you which bill to pay.

That is the list. No AI “debt destroyer,” no app that wants bank access, no model that will pick a balance-transfer card.
