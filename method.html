What FourHundred is

FourHundred is a U.S. money information tool for people already under financial pressure.

The name comes from a question the Federal Reserve has asked U.S. adults for years: if an unexpected $400 expense arrived, how would you pay it? That question is not a complete picture of anyone’s life. It is a public benchmark for a thin cash buffer. We use it as a starting point, not as a verdict.

FourHundred shows information people under pressure most need to see. It is organized in three layers:

  1. Foundations — core knowledge that changes slowly
  2. Playbook — current methods, with limits written on the page
  3. Evidence — numbers from primary sources, with the definition next to the figure

We also keep a screened list of AI-related knowledge and tools. AI is not the product, and it is not an adviser.

Who it is for

Adults in the United States who are already strained. That includes people who may miss a bill this month, whose card balance is growing, who do not have a cash buffer, who received a bill they cannot pay, who earn a solid income and still run out, or who are late or in collections.

It is not built first for people shopping for investments, crypto, or a “financial freedom” plan.

What a page is supposed to do

Every page has one job: help you see the next useful thing, not everything about money.

A Foundation page gives shared language and one small action. A Playbook page gives one method, who it is for, and who should skip it. An Evidence card gives a number, the exact definition, and what the number does not prove.

If a page cannot name its job in one sentence, it is not ready to publish.

How we choose what to publish

A page ships only if it meets all of these:

  • It is useful to someone already under pressure, not only to someone optimizing spare cash.
  • It can be explained with a dollar example.
  • It does not require us to know your full income, tax return, or risk tolerance.
  • It has a named limit: who should not use it.
  • Claims that sound like facts point to a primary source, or they are labeled as examples.
  • It does not recommend a specific bank, card, loan, investment, or filing.

We prefer official U.S. sources and established research over viral summaries. When two surveys use different questions, we keep the questions separate. We do not average them into one headline.

How the three layers stay different

Foundations are revised, not chased. Playbook updates weekly. Evidence updates when the source updates.

Home “This week” always carries one item from each layer.

How we treat data

Every Evidence card includes the number, the date, the publisher, a link to the primary document, the definition, who is in the number, what changed, and what the number does not prove.

“Cash or its equivalent,” in the Fed’s $400 question, means cash, savings, or a credit card paid off at the next statement.

How we treat AI

AI can help a reader understand a term already explained here, compare two published methods, sort a spending list with no account numbers, or draft questions. It should not pick a product, tell you to file, or receive live account access.

What we will not do

  • Give personalized financial, tax, or legal advice
  • Rank or sell financial products
  • Promise that a method will get someone out of debt
  • Treat a chatbot answer as a source

Corrections

If a number, definition, or date is wrong, the page gets a correction note with the old text, the new text, and the date of the change. We would rather show a scar than a silent edit.

To flag an error, email corrections@fourhundredx.com. See About for what to send and what to leave out.
