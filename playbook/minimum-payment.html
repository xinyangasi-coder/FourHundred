Why the minimum payment keeps a card balance alive

Last reviewed September 29, 2026 · 8 minutes

Who this is for. People who are current on a card, or trying to stay current, and whose statement balance is not going away.
Who should skip this. Anyone who already pays the statement balance in full, or who has an active collection lawsuit.

The method in one paragraph

The minimum payment is the smallest amount the issuer will accept this cycle to mark the account current. It is not a payoff plan. On most U.S. cards it is a small slice of the balance, plus that month’s interest and fees, or a fixed dollar floor. Because the slice is small and interest is charged on what remains, a balance can stay large for years while every payment is on time. Treat the minimum as a keep-the-lights-on number, then use the warning box on your own statement to pick a higher fixed payment if cash allows.

For you if…

  • You pay the minimum, or a little more, and the balance barely moves
  • A $400 repair or a grocery month landed on the card and is still there
  • You want to stay current without pretending the minimum is progress
  • You can read one statement and change one number this month

Not for you if…

  • Paying anything above the minimum would bounce rent, utilities, food, medicine, or a required car/insurance payment
  • The account is already charged off, sold, or in collections
  • You want a product pick — FourHundred does not recommend issuers
  • You want a personalized payoff date that uses your exact APR

Steps

1. Find the two numbers that matter. Minimum payment due and statement balance. Then find the Minimum Payment Warning box. Federal rules require issuers to show how long the current balance would take to clear at the minimum, the estimated total cost, and a comparison payment that would clear the same balance in about 36 months. Use that box before any article’s example.

2. Find the formula, not a guess. Common U.S. patterns are about 1 percent of the balance plus interest and fees, with a floor often in the $25–$40 range, or a flat percentage of the balance. There is no single federal formula for the dollar amount.

3. Separate “current” from “falling.” A payment that equals the minimum keeps the account out of late-fee territory if it arrives on time. It does not mean the balance is being retired.

4. If cash remains after essentials, pick a fixed extra. Read the 36-month comparison line. If that monthly amount still leaves rent, food, and required minimums intact, that is a clearer target than “pay extra when I can.”

5. Stop adding to the same balance if you can. New spending on a revolving balance is how a $400 shock becomes a multi-year balance.

A worked dollar example

Illustrative only. Look at your warning box for the account-specific version.

  • Starting balance: $2,000
  • Purchase APR: 22 percent
  • No new charges, no extra fees
  • Minimum estimated as 1 percent of the balance plus that month’s interest

Month 1 interest ≈ $36.67. Minimum ≈ $56.67. Principal reduction ≈ $20. Balance after the payment ≈ $1,980. You paid $56.67 to move the debt by $20. The account is current. The balance is still almost the whole problem.

That is what “keeps a card balance alive” means: on-time payments, slow principal.

What can go wrong

  • Paying the minimum late
  • Paying extra one month, then adding the extra back in spending
  • Taking a cash advance to make the minimum
  • Using an AI chatbot to pick a transfer card
  • Ignoring other bills to “kill the card”

Next action (10 minutes)

Write down: minimum due, statement balance, warning-box years and total cost, 36-month comparison payment. If the 36-month payment would threaten rent, food, or medicine, pay this month’s minimum on time and stop.
