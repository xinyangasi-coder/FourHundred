Why the minimum payment keeps a card balance alive

Last reviewed September 29, 2026 · 8 minutes

Who this is for. People who are current on a card, or trying to stay current, and whose statement balance is not going away.
Who should skip this. Anyone who already pays the statement balance in full, or who has an active collection lawsuit.

The method in one paragraph

The minimum payment is the smallest amount the issuer will accept this cycle to mark the account current. It is not a payoff plan. On most U.S. cards it is a small slice of the balance, plus that month’s interest and fees, or a fixed dollar floor. Because the slice is small and interest is charged on what remains, a balance can stay large for years while every payment is on time. Treat the minimum as a keep-the-lights-on number, then use the warning box on your own statement to pick a higher fixed payment if cash allows.

For you if…

  • You pay the minimum, or a little more, and the balance barely moves
  • A $400 repair or a grocery month landed on the card and is still there
  • You want to stay current without pretending the minimum is progress

Not for you if…

  • Paying anything above the minimum would bounce rent, utilities, food, medicine, or a required car or insurance payment
  • The account is already charged off, sold, or in collections
  • You want FourHundred to pick a transfer card or a loan

Steps

  1. Find the minimum payment due, the statement balance, and the Minimum Payment Warning box. Federal rules require issuers to show how long the current balance would take at the minimum and a comparison payment that would clear it in about 36 months. Use that box before any article’s example.
  2. Find the formula in the cardholder agreement. Common patterns are about 1 percent of the balance plus interest and fees, or about 2 percent of the balance, with a dollar floor. There is no single federal dollar formula.
  3. Separate current from falling. A payment that equals the minimum keeps the account out of late-fee territory if it arrives on time. It does not mean the balance is being retired.
  4. If cash remains after essentials, read the 36-month comparison line. If that amount still leaves rent and food intact, it is a clearer target than pay extra when I can. If it does not fit, stay at the minimum and protect housing first.
  5. Stop adding to the same balance if you can. New spending on a revolving balance is how a $400 shock becomes a multi-year balance.

A worked dollar example

This example is illustrative. Look at your warning box for the account-specific version. Starting balance $2,000; purchase APR 22 percent; no new charges. Month 1 interest is about $36.67. A minimum built as 1 percent plus interest is about $56.67. About $20 reduces principal. You paid $56.67 to move the debt by $20. The account is current. The balance is still almost the whole problem.

The Fed’s $400 question treats charge it and pay the card off at the next statement as cash-equivalent, and treats a carried balance as something else.

What can go wrong

  • Paying the minimum late.
  • Paying extra one month, then adding the extra back in spending.
  • Taking a cash advance to make the minimum.
  • Using an AI chatbot to pick a transfer card.
  • Ignoring rent to kill the card.

Official backing

Truth in Lending Act / Regulation Z requires a minimum-payment warning and a 36-month comparison: 12 CFR § 1026.7(b)(12). CFPB materials explain that paying only the minimum costs more and takes longer. Use your statement APR, not a national average, for your math.

Next action (10 minutes)

Write four lines from the last statement: minimum due; statement balance; warning-box years and total cost; 36-month comparison payment. If the 36-month payment would threaten rent or food, pay this month’s minimum on time and stop.
